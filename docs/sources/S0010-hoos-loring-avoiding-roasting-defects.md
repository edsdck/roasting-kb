# S0010 — How to Avoid Roasting Defects (Rob Hoos / Loring, 2020)

**Author:** Rob Hoos · **Type:** manufacturer technical paper · **Grade:** C · **Heat context:** multiple (written for convection Loring) · [Original](https://loring.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Loring-Tech-Paper-Avoiding-Roasting-Defects-1010764-RevA.pdf)

## Summary
A structured overview of roasting defects split into **objective** (visible on the bean:
scorching, facing, chipping, tipping) and **subjective** (taste-based: under-development,
over-development, "roasty", baked). For each: appearance, causes and avoidance. It openly says
there's no consensus on what causes "baked" and lists the competing definitions. Avoidance advice
for surface defects leans on the Loring's convection design, so it's partly promotional.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — Defects are either objective (physical marks) or subjective (flavour judgements).
   > "These defects can be categorized as either subjective or objective roasting defects." — *Roasting Defects Defined*
2. **C2** — Scorching: dark specks on the bean surface, usually before browning, caused by contact with metal that is too hot (charging too hot, too little airflow, too much early heat, thin drums).
   > "Scorching’s root cause is that the metal in which the coffee bean is in contact during roasting is too hot." — *Scorching*
3. **C3** — Facing: scorch on the flat face late in the roast, from beans pressed against hot drum walls as the batch expands (overfilled drum) or overheated metal late.
   > (paraphrase; see *Facing*)
4. **C4** — Tipping: charring at the germ end, usually early, mainly from roasting too fast; bean shape, density and altitude affect susceptibility.
   > "Tipping primarily comes from roasting the coffee too rapidly." — *Tipping*
5. **C5** — Chipping: small craters blown out of the bean, only in dark roasts around second crack; little flavour impact.
   > "This defect tends to only occur in darker roasted coffees" — *Chipping*
6. **C6** — Under-development (too short first crack → drop) tastes malty/nutty with aggressive acidity, metallic or vegetal; distinct from "under-roasted" (end temperature too low).
   > "Classically, this roasting issue is characterized by a malty/nutty character, aggressive acidity" — *Under-development*
7. **C7** — Over-development (too long after first crack) tastes muted in acidity, heavy, lacking nuance; distinct from "over-roasted" (end temperature too high).
   > "These are coffees which are frequently characterized by low or muted acidity, a heaviness, and lack of nuance or depth." — *Over-development*
8. **C8** — Baked tastes dull, lifeless, bready/malty, but there is no consensus on its cause: RoR crash after first crack, dip-then-rise (flick), stalled temperature, or long development (4+ min) with flat RoR are all proposed.
   > "Essentially, there is not consensus on this point in the coffee roasting community." — *Baked*
   > "Still others define it as a long development time (like 4+ mins) with a flat RoR." — *Baked*
9. **C9** — The Loring design relies mainly on convection, which the paper credits for avoiding contact-scorch defects.
   > "Loring roaster design relies primarily on convection heat." — *Convection Heating*

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
*Gatherer judgement:* The defect definitions and taste descriptions are machine-independent and
useful. Scorching/facing causes are framed around hot drum metal (conduction); on a radiant
machine, direct IR on bean surfaces is the more likely surface-heating route — not covered here.
The "4+ min development with flat RoR = baked" view matters because flat RoR is normal on IR (S0002).

## Gatherer notes
Manufacturer-published, so convection-favouring statements are partly marketing. Independently
confirms that "baked" is contested (see S0005, S0006, S0008).
