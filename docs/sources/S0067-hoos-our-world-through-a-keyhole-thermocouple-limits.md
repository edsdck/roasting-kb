# S0067 — Hoos: thermocouple limits ("Our World Through a Keyhole")

**Author:** Rob Hoos · **Type:** magazine article (reprint) · **Grade:** B · **Heat context:** multiple · [Original](https://dailycoffeenews.com/2019/07/09/our-world-through-a-keyhole-understanding-the-limitations-of-thermocouple-readings)

## Summary
Hoos puts thermocouples of three thicknesses (1.6, 3.2 and 6.4 mm) in the same roast and shows that the "bean temperature" is the temperature of the probe: turnaround, curve shape, rate of rise and end temperature all differ with probe thickness and placement, while the coffee is the same. He measured response in a boiling-water-to-air test and in roasts, and reports that moving from a thick to a thin drop-door probe exposed a negative RoR after first crack that explained occasional "baked"-tasting batches. His advice is to treat readings as relative and to check quality with colour, weight loss and cupping rather than profile shape alone.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — What the probe reports is its own temperature near the tip, not "the" bean temperature.
   > "the thermocouple is reporting its own temperature, specifically at and near the tip of said thermocouple"
   — *What Are We Seeing?*
2. **C2** — Thinner probes respond faster; thicker ones smooth the line.
   > "Typically speaking, thinner thermocouples are more responsive to temperature changes"
   > "thicker thermocouples tend to naturally filter the data, or smooth the line"
   — *Response time sections*
3. **C3** — In a cooling test (boiling water to room air) the thickest probe was still warmer than the others after nearly ten minutes.
   > "after nearly 10 minutes still registers 10 degrees F higher than the other two"
   — *Response time test*
4. **C4** — In one roast, turnaround came at 47 s (1.6 mm), 1 min 43 s (3.2 mm) and 2 min 46 s (6.4 mm), about a minute apart, with identical coffee.
   > "The thinnest thermocouple (1.6 mm) experienced turnaround in 47 seconds, the 3.2-mm in one minute 43 seconds, and the 6.4-mm in two minutes 46 seconds."
   > "this does not mean the coffee experienced anything different"
   — *Turnaround Time/Temperature as a Function of Response Time*
5. **C5** — Placement matters too, through exposure to airflow and radiant energy; the effect grows with batch size and widens toward the end of the roast.
   > "which affects the amount of exposure to airflow and radiant energy"
   > "Note that the 6.4-mm thermocouple placed in a different location gives us an even less responsive reading regarding changes in the bean mass."
   — *Thermocouple Placement*
6. **C6** — Around first crack the thinnest probe reacted strongly to burner changes and even dipped and rose again, while thicker probes showed a steady decline.
   > "In all the roasts, we see the 1.6-mm thermocouple react strongly to the burner adjustment around first crack."
   — *The Spread During and at the End of the Roast*
7. **C7** — Hoos's roastery swapped a 6.4 mm for a 1.6 mm drop-door probe; the thin probe showed a negative RoR after first crack on batches that were on profile by the thick probe and tasted slightly baked.
   > "the thinner thermocouple actually showed us going into negative RoR"
   > "We had been losing energy post first crack and"
   — *Practical example (Loring drop-door)*
8. **C8** — Readings are relative: good for consistency within one machine, not an absolute truth, and profile shape alone does not say whether a coffee is good.
   > "Remember that thermocouple measurements are relative and only a proxy for the actual bean temperature"
   > "At the end of the day, process temperature thermocouples are extremely helpful for consistency"
   — *Closing sections*

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
*Gatherer judgement:* Directly relevant to gap 4 (why first crack reads differently): probe thickness and position change turnaround by a minute and can hide or reveal a post-crack RoR dip, so the owner's FC and RoR numbers belong to the owner's probe only. Hoos also names radiant energy as a placement factor, which matters more on a lamp roaster where the tip can be hit by radiation directly (an inference, not tested in the article). The Skywalker's probe is not described.
