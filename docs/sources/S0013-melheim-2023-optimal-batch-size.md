# S0013 — Identifying the Optimal Batch Size (Roast Magazine, Sept/Oct 2023)

**Author:** Kat Melheim with production roasters/consultants · **Type:** trade-magazine feature · **Grade:** C · **Heat context:** multiple · [Original](https://www.roastmagazine.com/s/Roast_SeptOct23_Feature1_BalancingEfficiencyAndQuality.pdf)

## Summary
A feature on choosing batch size, built from interviews rather than experiments. Conventional
guidance is ~80 % of rated capacity at most and ~20–40 % minimum (Cooper uses 25 %). The
underlying advice: pick the batch size that lets you hit your intended times, and compensate when
changing batch size (charge temperature, burner, soak). Larger batches raise risks of surface
defects and uneven development; small batches risk an exposed probe and poor repeatability.
Probe readings shift with batch size.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — Maximum batch is usually suggested at 80 % of drum capacity; suggested minimums vary (20, 25 or 40 %). Both are starting points.
   > "Maximum batch capacity is generally suggested to be 80 percent of the drum capacity." — *Conventional guidelines*
2. **C2** — When scaling batch size, Cooper adjusts charge temperature by ~10 °C per kilo.
   > "Cooper recommends adjusting the charge temperature by 10 degrees C [18 degrees F] for every 1 kilo [2.2 pounds]." — *Scaling adjustments*
3. **C3** — Probe readings shift with batch size: one roaster finishes the same dark coffee at 440 °F in a full batch but 435 °F in a half batch.
   > "I might finish at 440 degrees F (227 degrees C) for our darker coffee in the full batch." — *Scaling adjustments*
4. **C4** — In small batches the bean probe must be fully immersed in the bean mass or readings mislead; small-batch results can be hard to repeat.
   > "When roasting smaller batches, you need to make sure the thermocouple or bean temperature probe is fully immersed" — *Risks of small batches*
5. **C5** — Larger batches increase risk of facing, tipping and scorching (especially single-walled drums, naturals/honeys) and of uneven development.
   > (paraphrase; see *Risks of large batches*)
6. **C6** — Under-batching increases the share of convective heating and may brighten a coffee — worth testing.
   > "You could, say, under-batch by a certain percentage and see if more convective heating will brighten up a coffee," — *Experiment carefully*
7. **C7** — Choose batch size from the profile you want (target times), not a fixed percentage; track yellow, first crack, development and end colour and verify by cupping.
   > (paraphrase; see *Practical recommendations*)
8. **C8** — Rao's ceiling estimate for drum roasters: ~5,000 BTU/hr of burner per 1 lb of green for a ~12-minute roast.
   > (paraphrase; see *Heat-source calculation*)

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
*Gatherer judgement:* Principles transfer (batch size changes readings and dynamics; compensate;
keep the probe covered), numbers don't (10 °C/kg is for multi-kilo machines; a 50 g change on the
Skywalker is a much larger *percentage* change). 350 g on a 400–500 g machine is 70–88 % of
capacity — at or above the conventional 80 % if 400 g is the true max. The "under-batch → more
convective" idea doesn't map neatly onto a radiant machine.
