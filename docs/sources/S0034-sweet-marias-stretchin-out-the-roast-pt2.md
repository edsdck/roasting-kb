# S0034 — Stretchin' Out the Roast pt. 2 (Sweet Maria's Coffee Library)

**Author:** Sweet Maria's staff (unnamed) · **Type:** article with roast logs and panel cupping · **Grade:** B · **Heat context:** electric drum sample roaster · [Original](https://library.sweetmarias.com/stretchin-out-the-roast-pt-2/)

## Summary
Second of three experiments in which one Rwanda Bourbon (Karengera) is roasted with deliberately different timing and cupped by two panels. Here the first crack itself is stretched by raising airflow earlier, with each roast ended about 30 seconds after the last audible crack. Five roasts were made; roast 3 stalled in first crack and tasted baked, so it was left out of the panel. The longer-crack roasts (about 2:00 and 2:28) had fuller, syrupier mouthfeel and sweeter finishes and were preferred by the panels; the shortest crack had the brightest, clearest acidity. Sweetness itself changed little. The author also gives a rule for avoiding baked flavour: never let first crack stop or fall below where it began.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — The author's view of first crack: the bean expands and releases moisture, and caramelization begins; if that stalls, baked flavours appear (harsh cereal, straw, sometimes corn).
   > "if caramelization stalls you can develop baked flavors in the cup – harsh cereal notes with straw and sometimes corn-like flavors." — *opening section*
2. **C2** — Practical rule when stretching first crack: do not let the crack stop or drop below the temperature at which it began.
   > "a good rule of thumb is to just be sure not to let the crack itself stop or let it get below the temperature at which the 1st crack began." — *opening section*
3. **C3** — The author places caramelization at an internal bean temperature of 340–400 °F, which depends on probe and roaster as a reading of about 380–415 °F (units are °F in the context of his numbers; the symbols in the text are bare degrees).
   > "between 340° and 400° INTERNAL bean temperature." — *opening section*
4. **C4** — Method: end point about 30 s after the last audible crack; airflow raised 15 s earlier (roast 2), 30 s earlier (roast 3) and so on; roast 3 stalled and was kept out of the panel.
   > "The end point for each roast was approximately 30 seconds after the last audible crack." — *method paragraph*
   > "Since I tasted the baked effects of the stalling on my first round of cupping, I did not include it in the panel cuppings." — *method paragraph*
5. **C5** — Longer first crack gave a fuller, more tactile mouthfeel and a longer, sweeter finish; the untrained panel picked roast 4 (crack about 2:28) and the experienced panel roast 5 (about 2:00).
   > "With these longer roasts, the mouthfeel was more full and tactile which created a longer and sweeter finish" — *panel results*
6. **C6** — The unstretched roast 1 (crack about 1:07) had the clearest and brightest acidity with a short, dry finish, and was both panels' second favourite; the author sees it suiting pour-over or as a blend accent.
   > "It had the clearest and brightest acidity." — *panel results*
7. **C7** — Roast 2 (crack about 1:23) was picked by nobody and the author found it flat and mild.
   > "The coffee that no one picked was Roast 2." — *panel results*
8. **C8** — Stretching first crack changed body, finish and acidity more than sweetness; the author links the syrupy mouthfeel to carbohydrates released in greater amounts (an attribution, not measured).
   > "Stretching out the 1st crack did not have much effect on the sweetness of the coffee." — *last paragraph*
   > "A more syrupy mouthfeel is related to the perception of particular carbohydrates" — *last paragraph*

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
