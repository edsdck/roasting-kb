# S0025 — Rob Hoos: How Cultivars & Airflow Change Your Coffee Roasting (Interview)

**Author:** Rob Hoos with Ingo (Roast Rebels) · **Type:** video interview, about 50 min · **Grade:** C · **Heat context:** multiple (drum roasters; he also says fluid beds show the airflow effect) · [Original](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YsZoyHmPigA) · Timestamps are from the video's English transcript.

## Summary
A follow-up interview three years after Hoos's first Roast Rebels talk. Hoos says his biggest changes of mind are (1) cultivar group matters more than he used to think and (2) airflow changes flavour and the weight-loss curve, which he used to doubt. He groups cultivars into Typica, Bourbon, Ethiopian and Hybrid, says Typica and Ethiopian types prefer faster roasting and Bourbon types slower, that fast is not always better, and that development time and first-crack time follow cultivar and processing while heat application follows moisture and density. He gives a weight-loss rule for light roasts and describes a fruit-forward natural recipe with a development time of about 1:00–1:10. Everything is stated from his own experience; no profiles, data or cup results are shown in the video.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — Hoos has reversed his earlier view that airflow is just a heat-transfer method with little flavour effect.
   > "I've always been of the opinion that airflow was just one method of heat transfer and so whatever your airflow was, it didn't really change flavor a huge amount. Um, totally disagree with that now." — *02:52–03:11*
2. **C2** — His airflow experiments found a change in taste and in the weight-loss curve.
   > "not only does airflow actually taste different, but there is an observable change in the weight loss curve of the coffee when you're using different airflow settings on a roasting system" — *03:11–03:29*
3. **C3** — He mainly studied drum roasters (the airflow effect is large there) and says it also exists on fluid beds.
   > "within the realm of traditional drum roasters, airflow makes a massive impact. It does in fluidized bed roasting too, but most of my research has been more on drum roasting." — *03:54–04:12*
4. **C4** — The airflow research is about airflow volume and its effect on inlet and exhaust air temperature; drum speed also changes the convection/conduction balance.
   > "the research is specifically around how much airflow you're moving through and what that does to the inlet air temperature and the exhaust air temperature and how all that kind of interrelates" — *04:39–04:59*
   > "the drum speed does change the convection/conduction ratios inside of the roasting system" — *04:39*
5. **C5** — "Faster is better" is a trend, not a rule: some cultivars suffer from very fast roasting, but can still be roasted very light.
   > "It doesn't always work out the best if I'm being perfectly honest. There are some cultivars that don't do as well with super fast styles of roasting." — *27:03–27:18*
   > "Doesn't mean you can't do an ultra light roast on them. It just means you can't do an ultra fast roast on them." — *27:12–27:23*
6. **C6** — Bourbon-group cultivars prefer slightly slower roasting; Typica and Ethiopian groups prefer slightly faster.
   > "it's probably going to enjoy a slightly slower style of roasting than one that's in the Typica group or in the Ethiopian group" — *26:33–26:45*
7. **C7** — Wrong match gives predictable faults: a Caturra roasted like a Gesha is thin and papery; a Gesha roasted like a Caturra is savoury and muddled.
   > "It's going to be very papery. It's going to be very thin" — *28:16*
   > "It's actually going to taste really savory and opaque and heavy and muddled." — *28:38–28:44*
8. **C8** — Hoos sets roast length, development time and first-crack timing from cultivar and processing; he sets heat application from moisture and density.
   > "how long should I be roasting? What should my development time be? Where does first crack hit? All that sort of question comes down to the two things of cultivar and processing method" — *34:24–34:38*
9. **C9** — Wetter coffee is harder to push heat into, so it needs more energy in the middle of the roast; drier coffee may need less.
   > "the wetter the coffee is, the harder it is going to be to push heat energy into it" — *34:38–34:49*
   > "you're going to have to push it a fair bit harder, especially in the middle of the roast, to get it to the timings that you want" — *34:58*
10. **C10** — Wetter coffee shows a higher turning point, may appear to run away early, then lags in the middle; turning the power down early makes the roast long.
    > "on a wetter coffee is your turning point's going to be a little bit higher" — *41:35*
    > "what I would generally expect for a wetter coffee is a slightly higher turning point and then needing slightly more energy in the midsection" — *41:56–42:05*
11. **C11** — Light-roast weight-loss target: 1–2 % above the green moisture content.
    > "generally for weight loss for lighter roasted coffees, I'm aiming for about 1 to 2% above my moisture content" — *35:24–35:38*
    > "if I'm roasting a coffee that's a 10% moisture content, I'm expecting 11 to 12% weight loss" — *35:38–35:45*
12. **C12** — His charge temperature follows batch size, not processing method.
    > "for the most part, charge temperature for me is based on batch size" — *40:24–40:37*
13. **C13** — His own "maximum fruitiness" recipe (natural Maragogype): smaller charge, faster airflow, yellowing at about 4:30, first crack at about 7:30, a development time of 1:00–1:10 because it is a natural, weight loss about 1 % over moisture.
    > "I'm going to try and get to yellowing at about four and a half minutes. I'm going to try and get to first crack at about seven and a half." — *44:03–44:12*
    > "because it's a natural, I'm going to probably have a slightly shorter development time. So, I'm thinking probably about a minute to a minute and 10." — *44:12–44:21*
    > "I'm probably going to run my airflow a little bit faster." — *43:58*
    > "hoping that my colors come out at about 70 whole bean and around 110 ground for espresso" — *44:21–44:27*

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
*Gatherer judgement:* Useful for gap #1: a leading consultant picks about 1 minute of development for a fruit-forward natural, which supports the owner's short development as a legitimate style, though Hoos reaches it with high airflow and a fast roast on a drum machine, which is a different heat path from the IR lamp. The weight-loss rule (moisture + 1–2 %) gives a target the owner can compare with his logged losses (the owner's roast logs); his green moisture is unknown. The airflow claims relate to gap #8 but are about air temperature entering a gas-type drum and may not transfer to a lamp roaster where air mainly cools (S0015, S0016).
