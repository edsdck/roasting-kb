# S0011 — Using Rate of Rise to your Advantage in a Production Setting (Anne Cooper / Cropster, 2021)

**Author:** Anne Cooper · **Type:** blog summary of a webinar · **Grade:** C · **Heat context:** gas-drum · [Original](https://www.cropster.com/blog-post/using-rate-of-rise-to-your-advantage-in-a-production-setting/)

## Summary
A practical take on RoR from a production consultant. RoR is a planning tool: set time,
temperature and RoR checkpoints tied to physical events (yellowing, first crack) and plan heat
changes ahead. Probe hardware and electrical noise shape the RoR line, so to match coffees across
machines you match **phase timings and end colour**, not curve shapes. A "perfect" straight
declining RoR line is not a quality guarantee. Pre-first-crack phases matter as much as
development, and development % alone doesn't define the end point — degrees after first crack
and colour do too.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — RoR is measured over a window (usually 30 or 60 s); 5° per 30 s = 10°/min. Cooper uses a 60 s expression so numbers read directly per minute for planning.
   > "an RoR of 5 over a period of 30 seconds means that the actual bean temperature is rising 5 degrees per 30 seconds or 10 degrees per minute." — *What is Rate of Rise*
2. **C2** — Probe type, placement, thickness, connections and power create different levels of noise that change how the RoR looks; understand your own hardware first.
   > "Understanding noise is as important as knowing your roast machine." — *Understanding your hardware setup and noise*
3. **C3** — Across three machines with different probes (1.5, 3, 6 mm), coffees tasted the same when phase timings matched; match phases and end colour, not curves.
   > "In testing, all coffees tasted the same because the timing of the phases were exactly the same." — *Differences in Probes*
4. **C4** — A roast with a "perfect" straight 45° declining RoR produced worse espresso (low body and sweetness, dry, tart, bitter finish) than a less "perfect" curve that moved more gradually through the early phases.
   > "Are you roasting the software or the coffee in the roaster?" — *Linear/Sequential cooking progression*
5. **C5** — Many roasters focus on development time and neglect how the coffee was roasted up to first crack; time spent in each phase matters.
   > "How long you spend in each phase matters." — *Linear/Sequential cooking progression*
6. **C6** — Development % alone doesn't define the end point: a 20 °F and a 30 °F rise after first crack can both fit a 20 % DTR. Define the end by time, degrees and colour.
   > "Roasting is all about time, degrees & roast colour." — *Anne Cooper’s RoR rate decision points*
7. **C7** — Example checkpoints (her espresso profile, 10 kg, 3 mm K-type probe): yellow at 5–6 min (160–165 °C), ≥ 4 min yellow → first crack, 3:00–3:30 first crack → drop.
   > "Get to yellow between 5-6 minutes (160-165C, 310-320F)" — *decision points*
8. **C8** — Near first crack, if bean RoR spikes but exhaust RoR is still declining, don't react — it avoids over-correcting heat.
   > (paraphrase; see *Rate of rise for exhaust*)

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
*Gatherer judgement:* Phase-timing approach and "don't roast the software" are directly usable.
Absolute checkpoint temperatures (yellow at 160–165 °C) won't match the Skywalker's lower-reading
NTC probe (S0001, S0009); phase *durations* are more transferable. Exhaust RoR isn't available on a
stock Skywalker.
