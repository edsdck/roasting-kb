# S0052 — Desnudo Coffee (2026): lowering fan speed on a fluid bed raised a blind cup score

**Author:** Desnudo Coffee · **Type:** roastery blog post · **Grade:** B · **Heat context:** multiple (drum and fluid-bed experience; test on a fluid bed) · [Original](https://desnudocoffee.com/blogs/roasting-1/can-you-actually-taste-the-difference-between-coffee-roasted-on-a-conduction-roaster-versus-a-convection-roaster)

## Summary
A roastery argues that machine type matters less than how energy and airflow are applied. It reports that, side by side, its Diedrich drum gave more body and sweetness than its Loring, and that fluid-bed roasting cut scorching and tipping. In the one controlled test it describes, the Sivetz fan was lowered as far as still fluidised the beans; the same washed Castillo roasted twice was cupped blind by a Q grader and the score rose from 83.25 to 84.5, with better body, balance and aftertaste. The post gives no roast times, temperatures or fan values, and one blind pair is not replicated evidence. The essay is the origin of the Home-Barista discussions in S0053 and S0054.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — Drum vs drum-with-more-convection: the Diedrich gave more body and perceived sweetness, the Loring more florals and acidity (same coffee roasted on both).
   > "The Diedrich (red profile) tended to produce more body and perceived sweetness" — *When the Difference Became Noticeable*
   > "The Loring (blue profile) emphasized florals and acidity" — *When the Difference Became Noticeable*
2. **C2** — Fluid-bed roasting cut scorching and tipping.
   > "Convection roasting dramatically reduces common drum-roasting defects like scorching and tipping." — *New era with the Sivetz*
3. **C3** — Reason offered: no super-hot drum surface, so heat application is gentler, provided airflow is managed.
   > "Without a super-hot drum surface, heat application is gentler and more even—if the airflow is managed correctly." — *New era with the Sivetz*
4. **C4** — A Roest owner (Christopher Feran) lowering paddle speed on a P3000 reported better body, structure and sweetness with less energy input (second-hand).
   > "Better body" — *A Conversation That Changed Everything*
5. **C5** — Feran's explanation: higher air speed raises convective transfer but needs more energy to heat the air, which then bypasses the coffee; he thinks the conduction share is unlikely to rise when airflow falls.
   > "Unlikely, based on drum temperature readings." — *The Geeky Part*
   > "Lower air speed means the coffee spends more time in contact with heated air and has a greater chance to absorb that energy." — *The Geeky Part*
6. **C6** — The test: fan lowered to the lowest setting that still fluidised the beans, same coffee, two roasts, blind cupping by a Q grader.
   > "I lowered fan speed to the maximum point that still allowed proper fluidization, lower than I’d ever gone before." — *The Experiment*
   > "Same coffee. Two roasts. Blind cupping by a Q grader." — *The Experiment*
7. **C7** — Result: score 83.25 to 84.5 with better body, balance, aftertaste and overall flavour (coffee: washed Castillo, 10.8 % moisture, 0.75 g/ml density).
   > "Cup score improved from 83.25 → 84.5" — *The Experiment*
8. **C8** — Conclusion: similar flavour is reachable on different machines; the method matters more than the machine type.
   > "It’s how you cook the coffee." — *So… Can You Taste the Difference?*

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
*Gatherer judgement:* The finding concerns a fluid bed, where air lifts the beans, so the "lowest fan that still moves the beans" rule does not map onto a Skywalker drum. What carries over is the method (one change, same coffee, blind cup) and the report that lower airflow was better on this coffee, which echoes the drum-roaster experience in S0053 and cuts against "more airflow is better" claims. One pair, one taster.
