# S0058 — Hoos: use the exhaust-to-bean temperature gap, not a lighter, to judge roaster airflow

**Author:** Rob Hoos · **Type:** consultant blog article · **Grade:** B · **Heat context:** gas-drum (as described) · [Original](https://hoos.coffee/blog/the-lighter-method)

## Summary
Hoos calls the old "lighter method" (a flame held 0.25-0.5 in from the trier) a fine starting point that oversimplifies and often leaves clients with wrong settings. He proposes comparing the exhaust-air temperature curve with the bean-temperature curve: a wide, persistent gap means airflow is too low (hot metal and inlet air, scorching and tipping risk); an exhaust curve that dips close to the bean curve is safe; one that hugs the bean curve may be too slow. A comparison of four airflow levels (25, 50, 75, 100 %) on his drum roaster is shown as charts only. His rules of thumb: make fan changes early (before 50 % of the roast), down-batch to 75-80 % if the roast is slow, and most people set airflow too low. No numeric temperature gaps are given in the text.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — The lighter test is a rough start; the air through a small hole near the exhaust cannot show airflow in the whole system.
   > "Since it is a significant oversimplification, we can not expect that airflow moving through a small hole (often located close to the exhaust port) can tell us how airflow moves throughout the system." — *What do I think about the "lighter method?"*
2. **C2** — He judges airflow by the relationship between exhaust and bean temperature curves, using bean, exhaust (immediately after the drum) and ideally inlet probes.
   > "we will examine the relationship between the exhaust air temperature and the bean temperature graph for clues as to whether we should increase or decrease our airflow." — *How do I dial in airflow?*
3. **C3** — Low airflow: wide, lasting gap; hotter metal and inlet air; scorching and tipping risk.
   > "When the airflow is low, there will often be hotter metal and hotter inlet air to accompany the hotter exhaust air." — *1 Too low…*
   > "if you notice that the exhaust air temperature doesn’t dip close to the bean temperature line, your airflow may be too restricted/slow." — *1 Too low…*
4. **C4** — A balanced airflow keeps the curves close; if that roasts too slowly, use a smaller batch or lower airflow briefly at the start.
   > "This is what a safe airflow looks like." — *3 Nice and balanced*
5. **C5** — On his roaster a 50 % batch with 100 % airflow all the way works well, first crack between 8:00 and 9:00; larger batches or faster first crack need lower air early and higher air later.
   > "It generally works well for me to run 50% batch and use 100% airflow (as pictured) the entire roast." — *4 Too slow… maybe*
6. **C6** — Inlet temperature should be limited to avoid tipping or charring, which is why an inlet probe is recommended; the four test levels are 25 / 50 / 75 / 100 % airflow.
7. **C7** — If down-batching to 75-80 % of rated capacity still gives first crack later than about 8:30-9:00, the heat source may not keep up with the fan, so reduce fan speed for part of the roast.
   > "then your roasting machine’s heat source may not be able to keep up with the air volume that the fan is moving through the roaster" — *Control Issues…*
8. **C8** — If at the end the flame is fully off and development still cannot be stretched, the fan may be too low.
9. **C9** — Timing and style: adjust the fan early, fixed or low-to-high is the more common pattern, and high airflow makes medium and dark roasts harder.
   > "I would recommend considering making your fan speed adjustments early in the roast (before 50% complete)." — *Control Issues…*
   > "it can be difficult to roast a medium or dark roasted coffee if you have too high of an airflow setting" — *Control Issues…*
10. **C10** — General observation.
    > "Generally speaking, I see more people with their airflow set too low than I do people with their airflow set too high." — *Closing thoughts…*

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
*Gatherer judgement:* The method needs an exhaust-air probe, which a Skywalker setup may not have (not checked), and the findings are for a fuel-fired drum with far more thermal mass. The portable parts are (1) his claim that more people set airflow too low than too high, which stands against S0040, where a very restricted airflow schedule fixed a green, tart natural Rwanda on a small gas drum, and (2) the check of first-crack timing after down-batching. Both are single-practitioner judgement with no numbers; the disagreement belongs on the gaps page rather than being settled here.
