# S0038 — Hacea Coffee's roast philosophy (Home-Barista thread)

**Author:** many members · **Type:** forum thread (20 posts over 2 pages, 2025-08-09 to 2025-09-17; both pages read) · **Grade:** C · **Heat context:** multiple · [Original](https://www.home-barista.com/roasting/hacea-coffees-roast-philosophy-t100506.html)

## Summary
A member summarises Hacea's four-phase "Roast Strategy" article (drying, Maillard from about 300 °F, caramelization from about 340 °F, post-crack development) and its stance that first crack is just an audible event. Members compare it with the three-phase view, discuss dropping before first crack (8–10 % weight loss, no grassy taste), and Hacea's Emily answers questions on Maillard time versus acidity, florals versus body, and why she now marks first crack at a fixed 385 degrees. It is a discussion of one roaster's theory; the evidence is the participants' own cupping memory, with a few numbers.

## Key claims
1. **C1** — The Hacea method splits the roast into four phases by bean temperature rather than three.
   > "Drying -> Maillard (starting around 300F) -> Caramelization(starting around 340ish?) -> Post crack development" — *post #1, Trjelenc (summarising the article)*
2. **C2** — Emily's observation: longer time in the 300–350 range goes with less complex acidity and aromatics; high heat means a short Maillard phase.
   > "Time spent in Maillard phase (300 degrees through 350 or so) seems to correlate inversely to perceived acidity complexity." — *post #12, emilymakescoffee*
   > "The high heat correlates to a short time in Maillard." — *post #12*
3. **C3** — Emily now marks first crack at a standard 385 degrees after one roast cracked at 389 and tasted sharp and bitter with little sweetness; re-marked at 385 it had a short caramelization and long post-crack phase, which explained the cup.
   > "have lead me to start marking a "first crack" time at a standard 385 degrees" — *post #12*
   > "The 389 crack coffee was sharp, bitter, and lacked a lot of sweetness." — *post #12*
4. **C4** — Emily's trade-off between florals and body: florals come from intense early heat and seem to be lost as melanoidins (body) build.
   > "floral notes tend to be created with intense heat during early Maillard (a short early maillard period)" — *post #19, emilymakescoffee*
   > "coffees with high floral notes tend to be thin or "tea like" in body" — *post #19*
5. **C5** — The article's recipes (as quoted by drgary): fast and short everywhere for bright fruit; short Maillard, longer caramelization and short post-crack for round sweet fruit; longer caramelization and post-crack to mute acids.
   > "For high acidity and bright fruit notes, I think about a short and fast roast where Maillard, caramelization and post crack development are all rather short in duration." — *post #18, drgary quoting the article*
6. **C6** — Dropping before first crack: drgary's IKAWA Home roast dropped just after 8 min, no first crack heard, 10.1 % weight loss with a 100 g charge; reaching first crack on another roast of that coffee lost the delicate origin flavours. EddyQ says others drop at 8–9 % loss and report no grassy taste.
   > "My moisture loss for my most successful IKAWA Home roast so far was 10.1%, with a 100 gm charge" — *post #5, drgary*
   > "I did intentionally reach 1C on another roast of that coffee and lost the delicate origin flavors." — *post #7, drgary*
   > "there are folks who drop their roasts before first crack. 8-9% moisture loss and they claim that are developed with no grassy flavors." — *post #3, EddyQ*
7. **C7** — Skeptical view: Maillard continues to the end, so a short "Maillard phase" may not be a lever; first crack is still useful as a probe check and a switch point to development.
   > "Maillard reactions we know continue through to the end of the roast, so I am not sure the short Maillard phase defined in the article is really one that can be modified" — *post #3, EddyQ*
   > "it does often validate your probes while roasting and points to a convenient time to transition to the development phase" — *post #3*
8. **C8** — Another way to standardise first crack: Theophilus marks it at a fixed 203 °C on an air fluid-bed roaster, where it usually coincides with a dip in the RoR.
   > "Now, I mark first crack at a standard 203 °C for most of my roasts." — *post #20, Theophilus*
9. **C9** — drgary's batch note: a 1 kg propane drum is fine for dark roasts at 1 kg, but a very light roast needs about 750 g to control.
   > "For dark roasts, it's truly capable at 1 Kg, but for light roasts, 750 gm seems to be what's needed." — *post #5, drgary*
10. **C10** — On IKAWA Home first crack arrives at an inlet setpoint around 253 °C (higher than bean temperature); drgary's no-first-crack profile hovers just under it.
    > "on the IKAWA Home, first crack is generally reached at 253°C inlet temp, which is higher than bean temp" — *post #2, drgary*

## Relevance to Skywalker (IR, 350 g)
*Gatherer judgement:* Gives the owner a second, named, small-roaster practitioner who builds roasts around bean-temperature phases with first crack fixed at a temperature, which is close to the "degrees above first crack" idea in the owner's roasts. Emily's phase-time cup effects are one roaster's perception, with no blind testing, and are from a gas drum, so treat as hypotheses for a 350 g IR machine. The pre-first-crack drop reports come from an air roaster.
